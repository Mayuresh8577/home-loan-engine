# Smart Home Loan Engine — Turning Transaction Data into Business

**A demo data science project built entirely on synthetic (fake) data.**

A bank already knows how its customers spend, what they owe, what they hold,
and where their money quietly leaks to other banks. This project turns that
into four answers:

1. **Who** is likely to take a home loan
2. **Can they actually afford the down payment** — predicted from spending behaviour, not just today's balance
3. **How much money are they keeping somewhere else** — estimated from the outflow trail
4. **What to say to them, on which channel, and which RM should call**

---

## ⚠️ READ THIS FIRST

Every row in `customer_data.csv` is generated by `generate_data.py` using a
random number generator. **No real customer data is used, needed, or touched.**

This is deliberate, and it is the right answer if anyone asks. Customer
transaction data cannot leave the bank's systems, cannot sit on a personal
laptop, and cannot go into any cloud or AI tool. This project demonstrates the
*method* without going anywhere near real data. If the bank ever built it for
real, it would be built inside the bank's own environment by the analytics team
with privacy and model-governance sign-off.

## The models, explained simply

Four different ML techniques are used. Being able to explain *why each one*
is the right tool for its job is the most valuable part of this project in an
interview.

### Model 1 — Classification: who will take a home loan?
**File:** `train_models.py` · **Answers a yes/no question**

Four algorithms compete on the same data:

| Model | How it works, in one line |
|---|---|
| **Logistic Regression** | Draws one straight boundary between "will" and "won't". Simple and fully explainable. |
| **Decision Tree** | A flowchart of yes/no questions: "Is FOIR under 0.3? → Is credit score over 750?" |
| **Random Forest** | 400 different trees vote; the majority wins. More accurate, harder to explain. |
| **Gradient Boosting** | Trees built in sequence, each fixing the previous one's mistakes. Usually strongest. |

**Result:** Logistic Regression won with **AUC 0.797**.

That's a genuinely useful result to explain: the relationships here are mostly
linear, so the flexibility of tree models bought nothing. And a bank *prefers*
logistic regression, because every coefficient can be justified to a regulator.

**What AUC means:** pick a random buyer and a random non-buyer. AUC is the
chance the model scores the buyer higher. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect.
0.797 is solid for behavioural data.

### Model 2 — Regression: can they build a down payment?
**File:** `savings_model.py` · **Answers a "how much" question**

This is the direct answer to your point that current balance isn't enough.

The model predicts **how much this customer actually accumulates each month**,
learned *only* from spending behaviour. Current balance, FDs and mutual funds
are **deliberately excluded from the inputs**, which forces the model to learn
from behaviour. A customer with a small balance today can still be a strong
saver, and this catches them.

Then: `months_to_downpayment = down payment gap ÷ predicted monthly saving`

This produces the pipeline view a sales head actually wants:

| Band | Customers | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Ready now | 5,279 | Call this week |
| Ready in 6 months | 234 | Start an RD now, loan later |
| Ready in 12 months | 225 | Nurture with a savings plan |
| Ready in 24 months | 514 | Long-term nurture |
| Not on track | 1,748 | Don't waste RM calls |

**Classification vs regression:** classification predicts a *category*
(yes/no). Regression predicts a *number* (₹18,400 per month). Different
problems, different metrics — AUC for the first, R² and MAE for the second.

### Model 3 — Regression: how much money do they keep elsewhere?
**File:** `savings_model.py` · **This is the answer to your UPI question**

We cannot see another bank's balance. **But we can see money walking there.**
Four visible trails:

- Regular self-transfers (NEFT/IMPS to accounts in the customer's own name)
- SIP debits to AMCs where we aren't the distributor
- Credit card bill payments going to other banks
- Insurance premium debits to other insurers

A Gradient Boosting regressor learns to estimate the hidden pot from that
trail, reaching **R² = 0.92** on synthetic data. (Real data would be noisier —
say so before anyone asks.)

That gives **share of wallet**: what fraction of the customer's total money we
actually hold.

| Share of wallet | Customers | With us | Elsewhere | Conversion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| We hold under 25% | 2,201 | ₹2.3L | ₹20.7L | 14.1% |
| 25–50% | 1,329 | ₹12.4L | ₹19.0L | 28.7% |
| 50–75% | 1,042 | ₹21.4L | ₹14.1L | 33.5% |
| We hold over 75% | 3,428 | ₹13.0L | ₹0.6L | 26.1% |

**2,695 customers transact with us but keep their money elsewhere** — an
estimated ₹581 Cr sitting outside. They trust us enough to transact, not
enough to save. That's the single biggest untapped pool in the base, and it's
a strong line in any pitch.

### Model 4 — Clustering: understanding the whole spending cycle
**File:** `segment_customers.py` · **Unsupervised learning**

The first three models had an answer to learn from (supervised). This one
doesn't. Nobody labels customers as "Lifestyle Spender". **KMeans finds the
natural groups by itself**, and we read and name them afterwards.

| Segment | Customers | Conversion | What to say |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Steady Saver** | 1,932 | 30.0% | You're already saving an EMI's worth — own instead of rent |
| **Wealth Builder** | 1,689 | 42.4% | Don't break your investments — loan against MF/FD for the down payment |
| **Banking Elsewhere** | 1,642 | 11.2% | Bring your balance here, get a preferential rate |
| **Lifestyle Spender** | 1,420 | 19.9% | Start a small RD — the down payment is closer than you think |
| **Stretched Borrower** | 1,317 | 13.1% | Don't push debt. Consolidate the card first |

Segments change the **message**, not just the target list. The same home loan
pitch should not go to a Wealth Builder and a Stretched Borrower.

## Step 7: the delivery layer — where business actually happens

**File:** `campaign.py`

A score sitting in a CSV changes nothing. This step produces the three things
that actually move the needle:

**1. App push notification** — what the customer sees on their phone:

```
Your rent is Rs 24,300. Your EMI could be too.
You pay Rs 24,300 a month in rent. The same amount could be an EMI
on a home worth about Rs 33L. You are pre-qualified for Rs 27L. Check now >
```

**2. Email** — same offer for customers who aren't app-active

**3. RM lead sheet** — what the relationship manager gets:

```
HOT LEAD  |  P1 - Call today  |  Branch: Whitefield  |  Best slot: 4pm-7pm
Rent Rs 24,300 (36% of income). Eligible Rs 27L at EMI Rs 23,200.
Down payment already funded (Rs 31L available).
```

The lead sheet carries the contact number, the branch (so it routes to the
right RM), the best time to call, the numbers to quote, and the talk track.

### The campaigns

| Campaign | Customers | Conversion | Trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rent-to-EMI Hot | 1,510 | 48.9% | Renter, down payment ready, high score |
| Home Loan Ready | 743 | 43.5% | High score, no rent (living with family) |
| Bring Your Balance Home | 2,201 | 13.3% | Low share of wallet, money elsewhere |
| Balance Transfer | 686 | 14.9% | Home loan elsewhere at a higher rate |
| Save-up Plan | 153 | 32.0% | Wants a home, short on down payment |
| Wealth / Idle Money | 158 | 21.5% | Large idle balance, no property intent |
| **Suppressed — credit stress** | 1,370 | — | High FOIR or card utilisation → no loan pitch |

### The rules it enforces

- Only contact customers who **opted in** to that channel
- **Suppress** anyone in credit stress — never push debt at a stretched customer
- App push if they're app-active in the last 30 days, else email, else RM call
- **Only hot leads go to a human.** RM time is expensive

### The result

**2,421 leads routed to RMs — 30% of the base — capturing 58% of all buyers.**
Conversion inside that group is 46.4% versus 24.2% across the whole base.

That's the number to put on a slide: *the same RM team, calling 30% as many
people, reaches nearly 6 in 10 buyers.*

## Why this beats a rent-only idea

Rent tells you someone **can service an EMI**. It doesn't tell you they can
fund the **down payment**, which is what actually blocks most buyers in India.

In the data, customers who can already fund the down payment convert at
**36.9%** versus **11.4%** for those who can't — roughly 3x. That's why the
project pulls in investments, liquid balances, savings velocity, and money
held elsewhere.

## Can we see transactions from other banks?

**Not directly.** If a customer pays through Google Pay or Paytm linked to
another bank's account, we can't see it. UPI is a payments rail, not a
data-sharing system, and NPCI doesn't hand transaction histories to banks.

**The legal route is the Account Aggregator (AA) framework**, regulated by RBI.
The customer consents through an AA (Finvu, OneMoney and others) for a stated
purpose and a fixed period, and only then does the other bank share data. Banks
already use this in loan underwriting.

**What we can do without AA** is exactly Model 3: infer the size of the outside
pot from money leaving our own account. That's our own data, not theirs.

## Honest limitations (say these before anyone asks)

1. **The data is synthetic.** The relationships were written into the generator, so the scores reflect the design, not proof the idea works in reality.
2. **Identifying rent from transactions is genuinely hard.** Most Indian rent goes by UPI or NEFT to a landlord's personal account with no label. Standing instructions and amount-plus-date regularity help, but false positives (money to family, chit funds) are real.
3. **EMI exceeds rent in practice** once maintenance, property tax and insurance are added. Messaging that pretends otherwise annoys customers.
4. **Privacy and consent.** Using transaction data for marketing needs compliance sign-off and must respect DPDP Act consent and customer communication preferences.
5. **Notification fatigue is real.** Frequency caps and a single-message-per-cycle rule matter as much as the targeting.
6. **No time dimension.** A real version needs a proper observation window and performance window to avoid leaking future information.
7. **Model governance.** Any real model needs bias testing, documentation and periodic re-validation.

## Likely interview questions

**Why Logistic Regression over Gradient Boosting?**
It scored higher here, and it's explainable. In regulated lending, being able to state *why* a customer was targeted matters as much as accuracy.

**What's the difference between the classification and regression models here?**
Classification predicts a category (will they take a loan — yes/no), measured with AUC. Regression predicts a number (how much they save per month), measured with R² and MAE.

**Why is clustering "unsupervised"?**
There's no correct answer to learn from. KMeans groups customers by similarity on its own; we interpret and name the groups afterwards.

**Why not use accuracy as the metric?**
The target is 24% positive. A model predicting "no" for everyone scores 76% accuracy and is useless.

**How did you avoid data leakage?**
Split first, then fit imputation and scaling inside a `Pipeline` so the median and scaler are learned from training data only. Cross-validation runs the whole pipeline inside each fold.

**What is `random_state` doing?**
It fixes the shuffle so the same rows land in train and test every run. `test_size` decides *how many* rows go to test; `random_state` decides *which* ones.

**How would you measure whether this actually worked?**
An A/B test. Half the leads go to the model-ranked list, half to the existing calling list. Compare conversion over one quarter. Model metrics are not business proof.
